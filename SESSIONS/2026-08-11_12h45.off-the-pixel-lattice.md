# Off the pixel lattice

Started 2026-08-11 12h45. Planned; nothing implemented yet.

Continues [2026-08-11_02h10](2026-08-11_02h10.shapes-colours-and-gradients.md),
whose phases 1–3 shipped. That session's phase 4 was "gradients as gradients". It
is still worth doing, but the evidence below moves it to third place and puts
something else first.

## The goal, restated

img2svg makes a **simplified** version of the source, not a faithful re-encoding
of it. Three properties, and every proposal here gets judged against them:

- **fewer colours**
- **smooth gradients**
- **paths with few anchors**

## The evidence

Two files sit in `examples/results-to-improve/`, both of the same album cover:

| | `cover-actual.svg` | `cover-better.svg` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| viewBox | 300×300 | **1200×1200** |
| fitter | `polygon` | `pixel` (`crispEdges`) |
| colours | 20 | 16 |
| paths | 557 | 513 |
| anchors | 7,503 — **13.6 per path** | 66,764 — **130.1 per path** |
| size | 37 KB | 183 KB |

`cover-better` is the older output and it is plainly the more accurate drawing:
the glasses are round with clean rims, the hair reads as strands, the eyes are
there. `cover-actual` has octagonal lenses and mushy hair.

Note what it is *not*, though: with 130 anchors per path and five times the file,
it is not the simplified drawing either. It is accurate because it is **big**, not
because it is well described. Neither file is the target; each has half of it.

### It was not a better algorithm

`cover-better.svg` was produced by the code deployed at **2026-08-10 15h38:32
GMT** — 17h38 local, so commit `0b307af`, after polygon fitting landed and before
splines. Checked what that code could and could not do: `convert_cluster` passed
`pixel_size: 1` and set the viewBox to the image's own dimensions, and the page
drew the bitmap at `canvas.width = bitmap.width`. Neither scaled anything. So its
1200-unit viewBox can only have come from a 1200-pixel input.

### `cover-better` is a genuine 1200 trace, not a scaled-up 300 one

Worth checking before drawing any conclusion, since a 4× scale would explain the
size and nothing else. Distribution of its `h`/`v` steps modulo 4:

| remainder | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| share | 4.0% | 68.6% | 19.4% | 8.0% |

and **66% of its steps are exactly one unit**. A scaled 300-unit trace would be
entirely multiples of 4 with no unit steps at all. So it was traced from a source
four times wider — sixteen times the pixels — while `cover.jpg` in the folder is
the 300×300 one.

### The bottleneck is the lattice, and one experiment shows it

The same 300×300 JPEG, upscaled 4× in a browser before segmenting, through the
**current** pipeline unchanged:

| | colours | paths | size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `cover.jpg` at 300 | 20 | 551 | 36 KB |
| the same file upscaled to 1200 | **16** | 3,115 | 366 KB |

The upscaled run produces round lenses, clean rims and separated hair strands —
it matches `cover-better`. Nothing about the palette, the regularisation or the
fitter changed. What changed is that **the contour was no longer locked to the
source's integer pixel lattice**.

That is the whole diagnosis. A region's outline is built from pixel cracks, so
every vertex lands on a pixel corner. On a 300 px canvas a spectacle rim is two
pixels wide, and a circle that must have its vertices on a 2-px lattice is an
octagon. There is no tolerance setting and no fitter that can recover a curve
that was destroyed before the fitter ran. Upscaling works because bicubic
resampling *reconstructs the sub-pixel edge position* out of the antialiasing that
the lattice threw away.

### The sharpest version of it: the tolerance ladder has no rung to stand on

Comparing the two 300-pixel runs at 8× on a single spectacle lens — same code,
same regions, only the fitter differs — showed something the whole-image views
hid: `pixel` tracks the curve of the lens, and `polygon` at 0.75 replaces it with
straight chords. The lens becomes an octagon. That is a defect in a default this
session shipped hours earlier, so it was measured properly.

Anchors on the cover against the polygon tolerance:

| tolerance | anchors | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.49 | 11,464 | |
| 0.50 | 11,419 | single steps flatten |
| 0.70 | 8,997 | |
| **0.71** | **7,704** | **−14.4%: diagonals collapse** |
| 0.75 | 7,503 | |

The two rungs are exactly where the geometry puts them: at `0.5` a lone one-pixel
step can be replaced by a chord, and at `√2/2 = 0.707` a 45° staircase collapses
into its diagonal. Nothing happens between them.

And here is the trap: **the edge of a small circle is a run of short 45°
staircases.** The collapse that straightens a long slanted edge — which is exactly
what one wants — is the same operation that turns a spectacle lens into an
octagon, because on the lattice the two are locally the same thing. There is no
tolerance that separates them. Below 0.707 arcs survive and slanted edges stay as
staircases; above it, both go.

So `photo`'s polygon tolerance was moved to **0.5**, just under the rung
(`PHOTO_POLYGON_TOLERANCE` in `cli.rs`, `PHOTO_FIT_TOLERANCE` in `modes.jsx`).
`pixelart` keeps `Fit::TOLERANCE` at 0.75, and correctly: there a unit is a pixel
*of the drawing*, so a feature is as many units across as the artist meant it to
be. The cover goes 36.2 KB → 43.0 KB and keeps its lenses.

This is a patch over the real problem, and it should be reverted once 4a lands:
on a large image 0.75 costs nothing and saves 41% of the anchors, so a single
absolute number cannot be right for both. Sub-pixel contours dissolve the question
by removing the rungs.

### And it is what is holding the curve fitter back

`spline` is currently a worse default than `polygon` — measured in the previous
session, it visibly cuts the corners off the lenses. The same comparison at 1200:

| | polygon | spline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| anchors at 300 | 7,503 | (no better) |
| anchors at 1200 | 85,878 | **62,976 (−27%)** |

Given a fine enough lattice the spline fitter beats the polygon by a quarter, and
given a coarse one it loses. It is not a weak fitter; it is being asked to fit a
curve to a staircase that has no curve left in it.

## What phase 4 should be

Three pieces, and the order is forced the same way it was in the last session:
each one makes the next worth doing.

### 4a — Sub-pixel contours

Place each boundary crack at the position the source actually implies, instead of
on the integer lattice.

The information is right there and currently discarded. Where a pixel straddles
the edge between regions painted `A` and `B`, its original colour is roughly a
mix `α·A + (1−α)·B`, and `α` says where inside that pixel the edge falls. Solving
for `α` per boundary pixel and offsetting the crack by it gives a contour that is
no longer quantised — the same regions, the same count, in the right places.

Why this and not upscaling, which is one line and demonstrably works: upscaling
multiplies the pixels by 16 and the paths by 5.7 (551 → 3,115), and it costs
sixteen times the segmentation work to recover information that was already in
the file. Sub-pixel placement gets the accuracy without either.

Against the three goals: it does not change colours, does not change gradients,
and does not by itself reduce anchors — but it is what makes 4b able to.

Open questions: what to do at a crack where three or more regions meet; whether
`α` is stable enough where the two colours are close in Oklab (the estimate is
ill-conditioned exactly when the edge is faint); and whether the offset should be
clamped to half a pixel to keep contours from crossing.

### 4b — Curve fitting that earns its default

With a sub-pixel contour there is a real curve under the outline, so `spline`
should become the `photo` default and the anchor count should drop rather than
rise. The measurement above is the reason to expect it, and re-taking it after 4a
is the check.

This is the piece that delivers **paths with few anchors**, and it cannot be done
first: today it makes the drawing worse.

### 4c — Gradients as gradients

The original phase 4. A ramp in an SVG should be a `<linearGradient>`, not N flat
bands. Detect groups of adjacent regions whose colours are collinear in Oklab and
whose geometry stacks along an axis, and emit one filled shape with a gradient in
place of the group.

It goes last, and not only because it is the biggest. Bands whose edges are
ragged lattice steps do not read as a gradient however they are filled — and 4a
is what makes their edges clean. It is also the one piece that reduces the
**colour count** as a side effect, since a ramp collapses from a dozen entries to
two stops.

Open questions: when a group of bands is a ramp rather than a drawing (a face has
collinear-ish skin tones that are not a gradient); linear only, or radial too;
and whether it composes with `gradient_step`, which currently exists to widen
bands and would become a way to *choose* them.

### Also, carried over from phase 3

`filter_speckle` is an absolute pixel count and so does not scale with the canvas:
4 is right at 300 px and far too small at 5 Mpx, and the 4× experiment above shows
it is what let the path count grow 5.7× when the resolution did. It should become
a fraction of the image the way `min_color_share` already is. Small, and it
belongs with this work rather than after it.

## What this is not

Not a fidelity project. Every piece here has to be checked against the three goals
at the top, and specifically **4a must not be allowed to raise the anchor count**
— a sub-pixel staircase is still a staircase, just a wobblier one. If 4a lands and
4b does not follow, the result will be a bigger file that looks the same, which is
the opposite of the point.

## Done in this session

## 4a landed — and it does not do what the plan predicted

Approved with *"ok, dale caña"*.

### Built

`src/subpixel.rs`. For a crack between pixel `p` (painted `A`) and `q` (painted
`B`), the edge sits at `crack + a(p) + a(q) − 1`, where `a(x)` is how much of `A`
is in that pixel's original colour — the projection of the pixel onto the segment
between the two palette colours, in plain sRGB, because what is being undone is a
*mixture* and the mixture was made on those numbers. Both sides are used, so one
odd pixel cannot carry the edge on its own.

The displacement is applied to **vertices**, not cracks: vertical cracks say how
much a vertex moves in `x`, horizontal ones in `y`. Rings stay closed by
construction, and because the shift is a pure function of the vertex, two
half-edges meeting at a node place it identically without having to agree.

Carried with it: the fitters now work in `Pt` (`f64`) rather than lattice `Point`.
That turned out to be small — `rdp_keep` and `turns` were already real-valued and
`spline` converted on entry — and it is exact for integer inputs, which the
snapshots confirm.

### It fixes the reported problem

The lens is round. That was the whole point and it works.

### It also costs bytes, always, and that is not a bug

Off the lattice, a horizontal run stops being writable as `h` with one number —
its two endpoints no longer share a `y` — and becomes `l` with two numbers and
decimals, about three times the bytes. Quantising the shift to a coarse step buys
most of that back by making a row of nearly-equal shifts *exactly* equal:

| step | bytes | anchors | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| raw | 89.6 KB | 10,856 | every straight run becomes `l` |
| 1/8 px | 82.6 KB | | |
| 1/3 px | 79.6 KB | | worse than 1/4: `0.333` does not fit two decimals |
| **1/4 px** | **75.1 KB** | **10,723** | |
| 1/2 px | 59.1 KB | 10,348 | steps visible along the arc |
| off | 43.0 KB | 11,419 | and the lens is an octagon |

An attempt to denoise by relaxing the shifts *along* the contour was written and
then thrown away: it made things worse (89.6 → 131.1 KB), because averaging
destroys the exact equality that `h`/`v` lives on. Quantising does the same job
the right way round.

### And on a large image it buys nothing

| `Sonic1.png`, polygon 0.75 | anchors | bytes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| lattice | 194,112 | 825 KB |
| sub-pixel | ~212,000 | 1,524 KB |

No visible improvement, +85% bytes. Which makes sense: at 5 Mpx the lattice is
already fine compared to the features, so the correction is mostly noise — JPEG,
texture, the palette being an approximation — added to a contour that was fine.

**So the plan's premise was half wrong.** It said sub-pixel contours would unlock
the curve fitter and *reduce* anchors. Measured: on the small drawing anchors fall
6% (11,419 → 10,723) and accuracy improves a lot; on the large one anchors rise 9%
and nothing improves. Sub-pixel is an accuracy tool for drawings whose features
are a few pixels across, not a compression tool.

Shipped **on** by default for `photo`, with `--no-subpixel` and a page toggle,
because accuracy on small drawings is what was asked for and the byte cost there
is 32 KB. That is a judgement call and it is the weakest part of this session: the
right answer is to enable it from the feature scale rather than always, and two
images are not enough to pick that rule.

### 4b is not justified yet

The plan expected `spline` to become the default once contours were sub-pixel.
Measured on the cover with sub-pixel on: spline at 1.5 gives 8,751 anchors against
polygon at 0.75's 7,647. Spline still costs six numbers per segment and this
drawing is corners, not arcs. It won by 27% on a *large* smooth drawing, which is
where it belongs. Not changing the default; the evidence points the other way.

Also measured and worth keeping: raising the polygon tolerance still destroys
small arcs even with sub-pixel contours (at 1.0 and 1.5 the lens facets again).
RDP is a chord simplifier, so on a 15 px radius a 1.0 px tolerance still spans an
11 px chord. Sub-pixel removed the lattice rungs; it did not make the tolerance
scale-free.

### Tests

- `tests/photo.rs::el_poligono_dibuja_lo_mismo_con_menos_datos` compared polygon
  against pixel by size. With sub-pixel on that comparison is meaningless — the
  two no longer draw the same line — so it now runs on the lattice, and the new
  `el_subpixel_cuesta_bytes_y_compra_sitio` pins the trade instead, including that
  `Fit::Pixel` ignores the shift byte for byte.
- `tests/trace.rs` converts to real coordinates to call `simplify`, which is now
  real-valued.
- All 16 suites pass; the golden snapshots are untouched for the fourth phase
  running.

## 2026-08-11 — 4c designed: a ramp is a colour that is a function of position

Approved with *"dale caña al 4c"*. The plan above says "detect groups of adjacent
regions whose colours are collinear in Oklab and whose geometry stacks along an
axis". Writing it down turned that into one condition instead of two, and it is
worth stating before any code exists, because it is what decides every constant.

### The criterion

A `<linearGradient>` can express exactly one thing: **colour as a function of the
projection of position onto a single axis**. So the test for "is this group of
bands a ramp" should be that same thing and nothing else — not collinearity in
Oklab, not a shape heuristic:

> A group of adjacent regions is a ramp when one linear gradient reproduces the
> colour of every one of them to within `RAMP_CEILING × tolerance`.

Collinearity in Oklab is then not needed as a separate condition, and it was too
strict anyway: a gradient with a stop per band can bend through Oklab freely. And
"stacks along an axis" is not needed either — two bands with different colours at
the same projection produce contradictory stops, the residual blows up, and the
group is rejected on its own.

### Stops per band, not two stops

The plan assumed two stops, which forces the ramp to be linear in Oklab and would
reject any ramp with a gamma in it. Instead: one stop per band, placed at the
band's centre along the axis, carrying the band's own colour. The gradient then
reproduces each band's colour **exactly** at its centre and interpolates across
the seams, which is what the eye reads as smooth.

This also makes the residual mean something sharp. Inside band `r` the ramp runs
from the midpoint with `r-1` to the midpoint with `r+1`, so the worst deviation
is **half the colour step to the neighbouring band**. The bound therefore limits
the size of the step between adjacent bands, not the width of a band:

- a genuine ramp has steps of about `tolerance` (that is what the palette does to
  a smooth ramp), so half-steps of about `tolerance/2`: accepted with margin;
- a flag of hard stripes has steps far larger than `tolerance`: rejected, and its
  hard edges survive. That is the case the criterion has to get right, because a
  gradient would soften edges the source has sharp.

`RAMP_CEILING = 1.0` to start, measured before it ships.

### Residual measured against what the renderer actually does

SVG interpolates stop colours in **sRGB**, not in Oklab. So the model evaluated
for the residual is the sRGB interpolation, and only the *distance* is taken in
Oklab. Measuring in Oklab a curve that is straight in Oklab would be checking a
gradient nobody is going to draw.

Sampled at the breakpoints: each band's projection range ends, plus every stop
that falls inside that range. Between two stops the sRGB path is a straight
segment and Oklab is near-affine over a step this small, so those samples bound
the deviation in practice. Conservative on the other side too: the projection
range comes from the region's bounding box, which for a band running across the
ramp is tight and for a crescent is loose — loose means a legitimate ramp gets
rejected, which is the safe direction.

### Merging is free, and it is where the anchors go

The union of a group of adjacent regions does not need any polygon algebra. Every
edge in `Regions` already knows the region on each side, so the group's outline is
just *the edges with exactly one side inside the group*, and `boundary::rings` —
which already turns a set of oriented edge uses into closed rings — chains them.
The internal band boundaries simply stop being referenced.

That is the piece that serves "paths with few anchors": those internal boundaries
between bands are the raggedest contours in the whole document, because they trace
where a smooth ramp happened to cross a quantisation threshold. A twelve-band sky
collapses from twelve paths and eleven ragged seams to one shape and one gradient.

### Shape of it in the code

- `src/ramp.rs`: one pass over `labels` for per-region moments (area, `Σx`, `Σy`,
  `Σx²`, `Σxy`, `Σy²`, bounding box), region adjacency from the edges, then greedy
  growth from the largest unassigned region.
- The axis: weighted least squares of Oklab on `(x, y)` gives a `3×2` gradient
  matrix; the axis is the dominant eigenvector of `GᵀG`, which is closed form for
  `2×2`.
- A group needs at least **three distinct palette colours**. Two adjacent regions
  can always be split by some shallow ramp, and that is an averaging trick, not a
  gradient.
- `Regions` grows a `ramps: Vec<Ramp>`, each with its own rings, stops and axis;
  the regions it consumed leave `regions.regions`. A `Ramp` is not a `Region` with
  a different fill — it has no single colour, and giving it a fake one to keep the
  vector homogeneous would be a lie in the type.
- Order of emission: ramps first, right after the background rect. Regions tile
  the canvas and shared edges are fitted once, so z-order cannot matter for
  correctness; ramp shapes are the large ones, and the document's convention is
  large first.
- Option `ramps: bool` on `ClusterOptions`, `--no-ramps` on the CLI, a toggle on
  the page. Default decided by measurement, not before it.

### What could go wrong, written before measuring

- A face has skin tones that nearly are a ramp. The projection consistency should
  reject it; if it does not, the residual bound has to.
- Cost. Growth refits and rechecks every member on each candidate, so a group of
  `m` costs about `m² log m`. Fine at fifty bands, not obviously fine on a 5 Mpx
  airbrush. A cap on group size may be needed; measure on `Sonic1.png`.
- `gradient_step` widens bands, which makes the steps between them bigger, which
  makes ramps *less* likely to be detected. The two features now pull against each
  other and the docs have to say so.

## 2026-08-11 — 4c landed, and the design above was wrong twice

Both corrections are worth more than the feature.

### Built

`src/ramp.rs`, plus a `Ramp` figure in `src/region.rs` — its own rings, axis and
stops, **not** a `Region` with a different fill, because it has no single colour
and faking one to keep the vector homogeneous would be a lie in the type. Option
`ramps` on `ClusterOptions` (default on), `--no-ramps`, a page toggle, and a
`, N degradados` on the report when there are any.

Merging cost nothing in machinery, exactly as planned: every half-edge knows the
region on each side, so the union's outline is the edges with one side inside the
group, and `boundary::rings` chains them. It only had to become `pub(crate)` and
learn the word *face* instead of *region*. Third time the half-edge IR has paid.

### Wrong the first time: one stop per band cannot fail

The plan's clever bit — a stop per band, so the ramp can bend through Oklab — is
one parameter per data point. The gradient then hits every band's colour at its
centre **by construction**, and a speck is short along every axis, so the residual
is near zero for anything. The criterion could not be falsified, and it wasn't:
on the album cover it produced 24 gradients out of JPEG ringing, two of which
smeared a face along a diagonal that does not exist.

That is the difference between fitting and interpolating, and only the first can
be checked. One stop per **colour** restores it: a colour must sit at one place on
the axis, and every band painted with it has to live with that.

### Wrong the second time: an absolute ceiling is not a criterion

Even per colour, it kept accepting noise. The reason is arithmetic: the six
near-black entries ringing a black stroke are all within two tolerances of each
other, so *any* model explains them within an absolute ceiling of two tolerances —
including a constant. The ceiling limits damage; it does not test anything.

What tests it is that the gradient has to **beat a flat colour by a factor**. A
flat colour reproduces any group to within the range the group spans, so:

> error ≤ `CEILING` tolerances **and** error ≤ range / `GAIN`

with `GAIN = 2`. The theory said 3 would admit any ramp of three or more colours;
measured, 3 leaves four gradients on a 5 Mpx drawing and 2 leaves twenty-four,
because real bands are not clean cuts of a ramp. This condition is not in the plan
above and is the one doing the work.

Constants were chosen by looking at the drawing, since the ceiling is a damage
limit and damage is visible:

| ceiling | cover | Sonic1 | what you see |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10 gradients | 5 | nothing, good or bad |
| **2** | **17** | **24** | identical to no gradients |
| 3 | 24 | 25 | the `l` of "blur" fades, two faces wash out |
| 5 | 25 | 22 | the yellow panel bleeds over the green |

### One hypothesis measured and discarded

I predicted the binding constraint would be the bounding box used to bound a
band's extent along the axis — loose for a curved band. Replaced it with the
support function in 8 directions (an octagon instead of a rectangle, 2% loose
instead of unbounded) and it changed almost nothing: 5 gradients to 5 on Sonic.
The bound is better and it is kept — it costs 22 ms and is the honest thing to
measure — but it was not what was blocking. What was blocking was that the
criterion had no teeth, which is the section above.

### Speed: 97% of the stage was work being thrown away

First run cost 1.9 s on top of 0.75 s. Not the merging and not the fitting: below
three colours the gain condition cannot apply, so a two-entry checkerboard in a
noisy area grew to the 256-band cap, cost its square, and was then discarded for
never reaching three colours. Cutting growth off at eight bands if the third
colour has not appeared took the stage from **1.5 s to 38 ms** with the same
gradients. Total cost of the feature is now 10%.

The band cap itself must stay high (256): a grainy sky is 121 bands of one ramp,
and lowering the cap to 64 takes that sky from 3 figures back to 23.

### It works, and not on the two images that started this

| | colours | paths | anchors | SVG |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| grainy sky, bands | 9 | 121 | 13,799 | 70.6 KB |
| grainy sky, one gradient | 9 | **3** | **1,854** | **5.9 KB** |
| `cover.jpg`, flat | 20 | 551 | 10,089 | 75.1 KB |
| `cover.jpg`, ramps on | 20 | 501 | 9,882 | 77.3 KB |
| `Sonic1.png`, flat | 21 | 7,158 | 295,433 | 1,904.3 KB |
| `Sonic1.png`, ramps on | 21 | 6,895 | 289,674 | 1,870.1 KB |

The sky had to be built to be measured — 900×600, a nine-entry ramp with
photographic grain — because both images in `examples/results-to-improve` are
flat-colour artwork. On the cover it correctly finds almost nothing and therefore
**costs 2 KB**; on Sonic it takes 2% off. That is the honest headline: this is the
right tool and these were not its images.

It also pulls against `min_color_share` from phase 2, which drops the palette
entries that paint little — and the middle of a ramp paints little. Turning it off
on Sonic goes from 24 gradients to 98, and still yields a bigger document, because
the finer palette costs more than the gradients save. Each wins in its own place.

### Tests

- `la_rampa_sale_de_una_pieza`: the synthetic drawing's vertical ramp comes out as
  one shape with one `<linearGradient>` and two holes for the flat blocks; the
  palette is unchanged, because this merges figures and not colours.
- `una_bandera_no_es_un_degradado`: four hard stripes stack along an axis and are
  still refused. This is the case the criterion exists to get right.
- `el_subpixel_cuesta_bytes_y_compra_sitio` now runs with ramps off. With them on
  the two documents are byte-identical — the only boundaries in that drawing with
  a colour mixture are the ramp's band edges, and the gradient just removed them.
  True, and not what that test is about.
- Six photo snapshots updated; `foto-por-defecto` goes from 10 flat paths to one
  gradient shape, two flat blocks and 3 paths total. All 16 suites pass.

### Suggested commit message

`A stack of bands is a gradient when one gradient beats a flat colour at it`
